# Earthquakes

Unlike wind and waves, which are daily operational loads, earthquakes are rare, extreme events. However, for tunnels located in seismically active regions, such as the "Ring of Fire" or the Mediterranean, seismic design is an important factor to consider to ensure the structural safety of the tunnel. The impact of an earthquake is heavily dependent on the specific geological location and the type of structure chosen. 

## Interaction with Floating Tunnels

A major advantage of Floating and Submerged Tunnels is their isolation. Unlike a bored tunnel or a traditional bridge, which are rigidly connected to the ground and must deform with it, an SFT is surrounded by water.

Water cannot transmit shear forces. Therefore, when the seabed shakes horizontally, the water (and the tunnel floating inside it) tends to remain stationary due to inertia. This effectively shields the main tunnel tube from the worst of the ground shaking.

## Critical Effects on the Structure

Despite this isolation, earthquakes still pose two specific threats that must be analyzed:

### 1. Anchor Point Displacement
While the tunnel tube is isolated, the **mooring tethers** and **landfall connection points** are not.
* **The "Yank" Effect:** If the seabed moves violently downwards or sideways, it pulls the tethers with it. Since the tunnel has high inertia and moves slowly, this differential motion creates massive, instantaneous tension spikes in the mooring cables. This may cause the cables to wear down or snap under the sudden load. 
* **Landfall Connection Point Shear:** The points where the floating tunnel connects to the shore (the landfalls) are vulnerable. The shore moves with the earthquake, while the floating bridge lags behind. Special seismic expansion joints are required to absorb this relative displacement without crushing the concrete tube.

### 2. Tsunami Hazards
In specific topographies, earthquakes can trigger underwater landslides or tsunamis.
* **Currents and Drag:** A tsunami in deep water is not a breaking wave but a massive movement of the entire water column. This creates extreme current velocities that can exert drag forces far exceeding those of normal tidal currents, threatening the lateral stability of the mooring system.
