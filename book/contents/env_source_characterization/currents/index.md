# Currents

While waves dominate the dynamic analysis of surface structures, currents can also be a governing factor for the global design of Submerged Floating Tunnels (SFTs). Unlike the orbital, oscillatory motion of waves, a current represents the steady, horizontal transport of the water mass.

Currents are generated by several mechanisms, including wind stress on the surface, density differences (salinity and temperature), and ocean circulation. However, in the coastal and fjord locations typical for floating tunnels, the current is primarily driven by the tides.

## Influence of Tides and Estuaries

Although the vertical motion of tides is discussed in the section about tides, their influence on horizontal currents is direct and critical. As the tide rises (flood) and falls (ebb), vast volumes of water must move into and out of the basin.

In "fetch-limited" environments like fjords, straits, or estuaries, this movement is heavily influenced by geography.
* **The Funnel Effect:** When a large tidal volume is forced through a narrow cross-section or over a shallow sill (typical in estuaries), the flow accelerates significantly to allow for the conservation of mass.


## Impact on Submerged Tunnels

For an SFT, the current acts on the entire submerged length of the tube and the mooring tethers. The interaction drives several key design limits:

### 1. Static Drag Force ($F_D$)
A steady current exerts a constant drag force on the tunnel, proportional to the square of the velocity ($U^2$).
* **Static Offset:** This constant push causes the tunnel to shift horizontally ("sway"). The mooring system must be stiff enough to resist this steady load and keep the tunnel within its alignment tolerances without slackening the upstream cables.

### 2. Vortex Induced Vibrations (VIV)

VIV is often the most critical hydrodynamic instability for submerged tubes. As current flows past a cylindrical tunnel, vortices are shed alternately from the top and bottom of the cross-section.
* **Lock-in:** If the frequency of this shedding ($f_s$) matches the natural frequency of the tunnel ($f_n$), a resonance phenomenon called "lock-in" occurs. This causes the massive tunnel to oscillate rhythmically perpendicular to the current (cross-flow), which can lead to rapid fatigue failure of connections and anchors.
